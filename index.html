Walking Together

An illustrated timeline of the First Nation–Farmer Climate Unity March: 94 miles from Des Moines to Fort Dodge, Iowa, September 1–8, 2018. Written for young Friends by Jeff Kisling, with his own photographs.

Wonder about it:

Before you begin

In September 2018, about thirty people walked 94 miles across the middle of Iowa. Nearly a dozen were Native Americans. Others were farmers, Quakers, students, artists, and people who simply cared about land and water. Most were strangers to each other when they started.

They walked along the route of the Dakota Access Pipeline, which carries crude oil from North Dakota across Iowa. Many Iowa farmers did not want it on their land, but the state let the company use eminent domain to build it anyway. Native nations, especially at Standing Rock, had objected that it put their water and sacred places at risk.

But the March was not mainly about the pipeline. As Manape LaMere explained, the purpose was to make it possible for us to work together in the future. To work together we needed to trust each other, and to trust each other we needed to understand each other.

Why would a Quaker walk 94 miles?

I grew up on farms near Bear Creek Meeting, just north of Earlham, Iowa. Every farmer is closely connected to the land. As a Friend, I believe there is that of God in everyone, and that if we wait in silence we can hear what the Spirit is asking of us. Then we try to do it.

In Indianapolis I had worked alongside Native people supporting the water protectors at Standing Rock, and praying together I felt a deep spiritual connection. At Iowa Yearly Meeting (Conservative) in 2017, the theme was Building Bridges, and I met Donnielle Wanatee and Christine Nobiss there. When the March was announced, it felt like a clear leading. I walked it at sixty-seven.

A day on the March

  • 5:30 a.m.The kitchen team makes breakfast and sets out food for packing lunches.
  • 6:00 a.m.Everyone wakes, eats, washes dishes, takes down tents, loads the gear truck.
  • 7:40 a.m.Morning circle: the day’s route, a reading, a prayer, or smudging with sage.
  • 8:00–3:00Walking, facing traffic, in bright vests. A break about every hour, lunch together.
  • AfternoonSet up camp, charge phones from the solar panels, help with chores.
  • 5:30 p.m.Dinner together.
  • 6:30 p.m.Community forum with people from the area, open to anyone.
  • 9:00 p.m.Quiet time.

Who took care of us

A rented gear truck carried tents and bags to each campsite and set up a solar generator. The sag wagon picked up anyone too tired or hurt to walk. A trailer carried two compost toilets and two solar showers. Volunteers cooked every meal.

Native leaders Manape LaMere, Alton Onefeather, and Lakasha Yooxot Likipt watched over our safety, driving ahead to look for traffic and storms and talking with anyone unfriendly. Manape called our walk a “sacred journey.” Everyone agreed to a code of nonviolence before we began.

Words to know

Queries for young Friends

For worship sharing, First Day School, or on your own. Let silence do some of the work.

  • Whose land do you live on? Do you know the names of the Native nations who lived there first, and who may still live there now?
  • Where does your water come from? What would it mean to you to say, “Water is life”?
  • The marchers came to trust each other by walking, eating, and telling stories together. Who outside your usual circle could you get to know this way?
  • When people disagree strongly, how can we stand up for what we believe while still answering that of God in everyone?
  • What could you share from your faith, and what would you like to learn from someone else’s?
  • Is there a leading growing in you, something you feel drawn to do even if it is hard?

What the March taught us

The March was never only about the pipeline. Its purpose was to build relationships so that Native people and farmers could work together on the things we all care about. That worked extremely well. In the years since, different combinations of the people who walked together have worked on many projects side by side.

Peter Clay, who represented Quaker Earthcare Witness on the March, wrote that the deepest lesson was about broken relationships, and how much healing will be required of all of us. He believed the March was part of the way forward, but that it is a long journey.

The knowledge I most needed, about how Native peoples have cared for this land and water, could only reach me through relationship. I had the reading and I had the hands-on work, and still that knowledge needed a relationship to travel through. Friendship opened the door.
